    GPU rental prices

    H100 listings range from $1.29 per GPU-hour for spot capacity to $11.07 for Google Cloud on-demand. The figures below compare different rental terms and configurations. Prices were recorded on October 13, 2026 unless noted.

    Rental terms

    On-demand pricing buys flexibility. Reserved pricing exchanges a spending or time commitment for a lower hourly rate. Spot capacity can be interrupted. Compare offers within the same rental model before comparing providers.

    AWS illustrates the effect of the contract term. Its H100 p5 instance is listed at $6.88 per GPU-hour on-demand and $2.97 with a three-year reservation. The lower rate comes with a longer commitment. Neither figure alone tells you which offer costs less for a particular workload.

    The four neocloud rate cards shown above list H100s at $3.90 to $6.16 per GPU-hour. CoreWeave advertises committed-use discounts of up to 60%, and Nebius up to 35%. Those advertised discounts do not establish the prices customers actually negotiate.

    Availability and price changes

    A published rate does not guarantee available capacity. Crusoe's $3.90 H100 offer was sold out when the prices were recorded. The $1.30 on-demand minimum refers to an in-stock single-GPU offer, while Lambda's and CoreWeave's chart entries quote a per-GPU share of an eight-GPU instance. Check the instance size as well as the hourly rate.

    Different price series cover different markets. SemiAnalysis reports that one-year H100 rental contracts rose from $1.70 per GPU-hour in October 2025 to $2.35 in March 2026, an increase of 38%. GetDeploying's on-demand median was flat over the 90 days preceding this snapshot. The series cover different rental terms and periods, so neither describes every buyer's price.

    Cost per completed job

    An hourly rate becomes useful when you know how many billed hours a job needs. Compare the full instance charge, runtime, and any storage or data-transfer charges. For reserved capacity, count the hours you pay for but do not use. For spot capacity, include the work lost to interruptions.

    Consider a simplified example. At $2.50 per reserved GPU-hour, using only half the hours you pay for raises the compute cost to $5.00 per used GPU-hour. If failures also consume 8% of those used hours, the cost reaches about $5.43 per useful GPU-hour. These figures assume idle and failed hours remain billable and exclude other charges.

    01

    What a neocloud is

    A neocloud is a cloud provider whose main business is renting graphics processing units (GPUs) or other AI accelerators. Its service combines accelerator hardware with the networking, storage, and software needed to run AI workloads. Hyperscalers such as AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, and Oracle offer accelerator capacity within a much broader cloud business.

    The directory includes specialist GPU clouds and related businesses, including data center developers, marketplaces, and AI platforms. GetDeploying lists 56 providers renting H100 GPUs in the October 2026 snapshot. Providers such as Fluidstack and Nscale sell through contracts without publishing hourly prices. The public rate cards therefore cover only part of the market.

    AI factories

    NVIDIA uses the term AI factory for infrastructure that supports data preparation, model training, and inference, the process of running a trained model. The label also appears in descriptions of different businesses. Firmus uses it for GPU capacity sold to Meta, Applied Digital for campuses leased under 15-year contracts, and Deutsche Telekom for an industrial AI cloud. To understand an offer, identify whether the company supplies buildings, operates hardware, or delivers a software service.

    Service models

    Providers sell different levels of service. Bare metal gives customers control of the servers. Managed clusters add operational software and support. Inference and other GPU services let customers buy a more specific task. Compare the responsibilities included in each offer before comparing prices.

    Bare metal

    Dedicated servers or clusters. The customer manages the workload software and takes responsibility for more of the system.

    Suited to teams that need direct hardware access and have the staff to operate the software stack.

    Managed cluster

    A cluster with scheduling, storage, monitoring, and operational support. Slurm and Kubernetes are common systems for scheduling workloads.

    The provider and customer divide responsibility for provisioning, maintenance, and recovery. The contract defines that division.

    Inference and GPU services

    Model inference APIs, serverless execution, fine-tuning, and related services. Customers interact with a service rather than manage each server.

    Pricing may follow tokens, requests, or execution time instead of a reserved GPU-hour.

    Financing and operating costs

    A provider must pay for servers and facilities before the capacity earns revenue. Long-term customer contracts can support borrowing, while customer prepayments and equity reduce the cash the provider must raise elsewhere. Major agreements include Meta's contracts with CoreWeave and Nebius, and Microsoft's with Nebius and IREN. Financing still depends on the contract terms, collateral, customer, and provider.

    Operating costs and asset life

    Utilization and goodput

    Utilization measures how much capacity runs workloads. Goodput measures the useful work delivered after failures and other losses. Idle servers and slow recovery can reduce returns, although the effect on billed revenue depends on the contract.

    Depreciation life

    Accounting schedules and commercial usefulness measure different things. CoreWeave depreciates GPUs over six years, Lambda over five, and Nebius over four. CoreWeave also reports an A100 contract extending into 2029, nine years after the chip launched. Hardware resale value and customer demand still depend on the equipment and workload.

    Power

    A power contract reserves future supply. An energized site can receive electricity. Neither figure alone establishes how much compute is ready for customers. CoreWeave reported 1.5 GW of active power and 4.2 GW contracted by August 2026. One gigawatt (GW) equals 1,000 megawatts (MW).

    Product mix

    Bare-metal contracts, managed clusters, and inference services have different staffing costs, billing models, and commitments. Compare operating costs and customer usage alongside the rate card.

    Owned or leased buildings

    Facility ownership and operating responsibility can differ. Establish who maintains cooling and power, who replaces failed components, and whether technicians and spare parts are available at the site.

    04

    Provider comparison

    Two offers for the same GPU model can deliver different runtimes and total costs. Compare the complete system and the service agreement, then test a workload that represents your own use. The checklist below is an evaluation framework, rather than a ranking of the providers in the directory.

    Hardware identity and health

    Confirm the exact accelerator model, memory capacity, and form factor. Check hardware health and thermal behavior, then inspect the host CPU, memory, and local storage that feed the accelerators.

    Interconnect and placement

    Check connections within each server and across the cluster. Measure communication under load, including the operations your training job uses to combine results across GPUs. A network's advertised link speed does not establish application throughput.

    Reliability and interruptions

    Ask about hardware failures, planned maintenance, and interruptions the provider may initiate. Measure how much work a job loses and how long recovery takes, including checkpoint loading and rescheduling.

    Capacity and timing

    Confirm when capacity becomes available and how many GPUs can run together. A job that needs 1,000 simultaneous GPUs cannot use the same number of GPU-hours spread across smaller allocations.

    Software and platform

    Check supported drivers, accelerator libraries, scheduling tools, and storage performance. Include provisioning and startup time in a trial. Test the software versions and cluster size you expect to use.

    Location and jurisdiction

    Check the distance from your data and users, the permitted location of stored data, and any restrictions on access or deployment. Evaluate the specific region offered in the quote.

    Contract terms

    Read the minimum commitment, billing increments, transfer charges, and support terms. Identify the remedies and limits in the service-level agreement (SLA), including how the provider measures outages.

    Acceptance tests and measurement

    Agree on acceptance tests and access to measurements before the contract starts. Use your own workload where possible, and record the configuration so later results remain comparable.

    Provider questions

    • Can we run a full-cluster communication test and our own workload before billing starts?
    • How long does fault detection and replacement take for a GPU, network adapter, or link? Which spare parts stay on site?
    • Who operates the building and performs repairs outside normal working hours?
    • Which software and firmware versions will we use, and how does the provider test and deploy updates?
    • Can the provider interrupt the allocation, and what remedies apply if capacity arrives late?
    • How does the service-level agreement measure outages, calculate credits, and limit compensation?
    • What storage throughput can our job sustain when the full cluster reads or writes at once?
    • Which security assessments cover the service, and what findings can the provider share?

    Contracts, investment, and security

    Customer concentration

    The customer lists show repeated relationships with a small group of buyers. In the network, Anthropic and NVIDIA each appear as customers of 11 providers, while Microsoft and OpenAI each appear in 8 lists. Those counts measure disclosed relationships, not revenue shares.

    Long contracts with large buyers

    Meta announced a $21B expansion with CoreWeave and an agreement worth up to $27B with Nebius in spring 2026. Multi-year contract values spread across future delivery periods. They do not equal current annual revenue.

    Public and private pricing

    Fluidstack and Nscale do not publish hourly rates, while GetDeploying lists H100 spot capacity from $1.29 per hour. Public listings describe the capacity offered through those channels. They do not reveal the prices or volume in private contracts.

    Mining sites and AI capacity

    Former miners in the directory are developing AI hosting or cloud businesses using their sites and power access. Hut 8 reports leasing its full 1 GW Beacon Point campus in Texas for $19.6B of base-term contract value. A signed lease and an operating GPU cluster represent different milestones.

    Funding and valuations

    Crusoe raised funding at a $30.9B valuation in September 2026. Firmus nearly doubled its valuation in four months before withdrawing its planned October listing. Nscale filed for a US listing in September with $103.4B of contracted value. Equity valuations and customer contract values measure different things.

    Public-market caution

    Redburn began coverage of CoreWeave and Nebius with Sell ratings on September 21, 2026, arguing that GPU rental prices would fall. Firmus later withdrew its ASX listing after weak investor demand. Those events show investor concerns alongside the large funding rounds and contracts.

    Security under scrutiny

    In July 2025, Wiz disclosed NVIDIAScape, a vulnerability in the NVIDIA Container Toolkit that could let a malicious container gain control of its host and access other customers' data. The incident illustrates why shared infrastructure requires attention to isolation and software maintenance.

    06

    Industry developments

    Hardware announcements, new services, and financing agreements describe plans as well as operating businesses. Follow their delivery milestones, customer adoption, and reported results. The developments below identify concrete changes to track rather than predict a single outcome for the industry.

    Technology

    Vera Rubin NVL72

    Rack power rises from roughly 120 to 140 kW for Grace Blackwell to 180 to 220 kW for Vera Rubin, with an 800 VDC power option. NVIDIA specifies 1.6 Tb/s of scale-out bandwidth per GPU through ConnectX-9. CoreWeave reported its first Vera Rubin NVL72 bring-up in June 2026. Initial validation and broad customer availability are separate milestones.

    Scale-out networking

    Large clusters can distribute traffic across multiple independent network paths. OpenAI released its MRC transport protocol for these networks through the Open Compute Project in May 2026, working with AMD, Broadcom, Intel, Microsoft, and NVIDIA. Track deployment and measured cluster performance alongside protocol announcements.

    AMD

    TensorWave is adopting AMD MI455X Helios rack-scale systems, and Oracle plans a 50,000-GPU MI450 cluster. Compare complete workloads, including software compatibility and operating effort, when evaluating an alternative accelerator platform.

    TPU capacity outside Google

    Google is selling TPUs to outside customers. Blackstone and Google announced a TPU cloud with $5B of equity and about 500 MW planned for 2027. Anthropic plans to access about 3.5 GW of TPU capacity through Broadcom from 2027. Track the delivery dates and service terms of those projects.

    Products

    Inference endpoints

    CoreWeave reports that booked annual recurring revenue (ARR) for managed inference rose from $1M to more than $100M in a few months. Crusoe reports over $100M of contracted inference ARR. Nebius acquired Eigen AI and Inferize in 2026 for its Token Factory platform. Booked or contracted ARR does not equal revenue already earned.

    Post-training and reinforcement learning

    Crusoe launched serverless fine-tuning in July 2026. Post-training services extend the offer beyond renting servers. Evaluate the supported models, execution environment, and billing terms as part of the service.

    Smaller inference sites

    CNBC reported in September 2026 that Anthropic and OpenAI were seeking 20 to 30 MW deployments in the UK, the Nordics, and the US alongside larger campuses. Smaller sites may serve some inference workloads without the single-site scale required by large training runs. Their usefulness still depends on the model, workload, and network.

    Reliability tooling

    NVIDIA open-sourced NVSentinel for GPU fault detection and remediation on Kubernetes in October 2025. Evaluate whether operational tooling reduces detection time, recovery time, and lost work in the deployed system.

    Capital and policy

    Financing terms

    Google-backed leases, vendor rack leasing, and NVIDIA equity investments appear in major 2025 and 2026 agreements. Follow who funds each project, who guarantees payments, and which party bears the loss if delivery or demand falls short.

    Rental price indices

    SemiAnalysis and Silicon Data publish H100 rental indices. Compare their rental terms, hardware coverage, and observation periods before treating them as measures of the same market.

    Geography

    CoreWeave entered Indonesia in August 2026, and Firmus added OpenAI as an anchor customer in Malaysia. State-backed programs also support capacity purchases in Korea, India, and Saudi Arabia. Separate announced projects from available capacity in each region.

    08

    Glossary

    Bare metal

    Dedicated physical servers that a customer rents and controls. The provider may still supply provisioning, networking, monitoring, or support.

    Colocation

    Space, electricity, and cooling rented in a data center for hardware that the tenant supplies or operates.

    Delayed-draw term loan (DDTL)

    A loan that the borrower draws in stages as project spending occurs, rather than receiving the full amount at once.

    Goodput

    The rate of useful work a system completes after accounting for failures, restarts, and other overhead. Compare definitions when reading reported goodput figures.

    Hyperscaler

    A large general-purpose cloud provider, such as AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, or Oracle Cloud Infrastructure.

    Model FLOPs utilization (MFU)

    The arithmetic work required by a model's training computation divided by the hardware's theoretical peak throughput over the same period. MFU helps describe how effectively a training run uses its accelerators.

    Neolab

    A newer AI model developer. The term does not specify a standard company size, product, or infrastructure model.

    NVL72

    An NVIDIA rack configuration connecting 72 GPUs through NVLink. NVL72 appears in the GB200, GB300, and Vera Rubin product families.

    Offtaker

    A customer that commits to purchase capacity. The provider may use the customer contract to support project financing.

    Remaining performance obligations (RPO)

    Contracted revenue that a company has not yet recognized under its reporting rules. RPO and a company's stated backlog can use different definitions.

    SXM and PCIe

    Accelerator form factors. SXM modules attach to a server baseboard, while PCIe cards use expansion slots. Check the specific product's memory, power, and interconnect configuration.

    Take-or-pay

    A contract requiring payment for committed capacity even when the customer does not use it, subject to the agreement's delivery and other terms.

    Method

    The guide draws on company filings, announcements, and reporting available through October 13, 2026. Numbered citations link claims and figures to the listed sources. Company announcements describe the company's stated position, while reporting may describe plans or agreements that the company has not confirmed.

    Contracted, planned, and operating capacity describe different stages of a project. Backlog, contract value, annual recurring revenue, and recognized revenue are different measures. Read company figures using the period and definition attached to each claim.

    The network connects providers to the customers named in their profiles. Customers outside the directory appear when at least two providers name them. A link records a disclosed relationship, without measuring its revenue or capacity. The map marks headquarters rather than data center locations, and logos identify the companies.

    The Prices section uses public rate cards and pricing trackers recorded on October 13, 2026 unless an entry gives another date. Prices and availability can change. Open the source list at the end of a section or follow a numbered citation to inspect its references.

    The provider checklist, segment comparisons, and risk explanations are the author's analysis. Profiles with a limited public record identify that limitation. Send corrections to sonnet@stanford.edu with a supporting source and the claim that needs updating.
